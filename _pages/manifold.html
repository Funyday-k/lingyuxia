Why does general relativity need this?

Manifold M
Spacetime is a collection of events. Near any event, four independent coordinates label where and when it happens.
Metric g
Coordinates are labels. The metric connects coordinate differences to the spacetime intervals measured by clocks and rulers.
Curvature tensor
A freely falling frame can remove the connection at a point, but generally not tidal effects: neighboring freely falling bodies can still accelerate relative to one another.
From intuition to definitions

Locally Euclidean does not mean geometrically flat

An n-dimensional topological manifold without boundary is a Hausdorff, second-countable topological space in which every point has an open neighborhood U homeomorphic to an open set V ⊂ ℝⁿ. A homeomorphism φ: U → V is a continuous bijection with a continuous inverse. Hausdorff means distinct points have disjoint neighborhoods; second-countable means there is a countable basis for the topology.

The first scene uses the projection φ(x,y,z)=(x,y) on the upper unit sphere, with inverse (u,v) ↦ (u,v,√(1−u²−v²)). The blue region z > cos α maps to the open disk u²+v² < sin²α. The animation shows a correspondence: it neither preserves distances and areas nor physically flattens the sphere.

Charts, atlases and smoothness

A coordinate chart is the pair (U,φ). An atlas is a collection of charts whose domains cover the manifold. In the third scene, removing any of the four open semicircle charts leaves at least one point uncovered. Larger open sets allow the circle to be covered by just two charts. One chart cannot cover the circle: the circle is compact, while a nonempty open subset of ℝ is not.

On an overlap, the transition map is φβ ∘ φα⁻¹. An atlas is smooth when its transition maps are C∞; compatible atlases determine a smooth structure. The fourth scene uses the first-quadrant overlap of the upper and right charts. The transition map and its inverse are smooth on (0,1). Boundary points are excluded.

Why do both charts give the same distance?

Write the track as x=cos θ, y=sin θ, with radius R=1 m. The dimensionless coordinates are u=x in the upper chart and v=y in the right chart. Their metric components are gᵤᵤ=R²/(1−u²) and gᵥᵥ=R²/(1−v²). Both describe the same line element dℓ=R|dθ|.

Finite distances shown here come from the exact integral ℓ=R|arcsin u₂−arcsin u₁|=R|arcsin v₂−arcsin v₁|=RΔθ, provided the entire segment lies inside each chart. A finite coordinate difference is not substituted for a differential. Near a chart boundary a metric component may diverge, even though the circular track remains regular.

The circle illustrates coordinates and length. Its bending in the drawing is extrinsic: every one-dimensional Riemannian manifold has zero intrinsic Riemann curvature. The sphere in the first scene has nonzero Gaussian curvature K=1/R². Shrinking a neighborhood improves the local flat approximation without changing K.

From manifolds to general relativity

Dimension counts the independent coordinates needed locally: one for a circle, two for a sphere. Drawing a manifold in three-dimensional space is a visual aid; its definition requires no surrounding space. General relativity equips a four-dimensional smooth manifold with a Lorentzian metric to describe spacetime intervals and causal structure. A manifold alone specifies no lengths, angles or curvature.

“Locally Euclidean” refers to topology and coordinates, not a Euclidean spacetime metric. At a point, local inertial coordinates can make the metric Minkowskian and the connection vanish. Curvature can remain nonzero, and a coordinate change cannot remove tidal effects throughout a finite region.
